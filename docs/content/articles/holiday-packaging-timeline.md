# Holiday Packaging Timeline: Plan Backwards from Your Launch

Holiday packaging has one fixed point: the day your product has to be on the shelf, in the online store or in your customer's hands. Everything else moves around that date. The brands that get seasonal packaging right are rarely the ones with the biggest budget; they are the ones that start from the launch date, work backwards through every step and leave room for the steps that usually slip.

This guide covers seasonal packaging for retail and brand products: Christmas and New Year gift boxes, advent calendars and limited-edition packs. If you are planning gifts for clients and employees, see our [corporate gift packaging planning guide](/en/articles/corporate-gift-packaging-planning-guide).

## Start from the date that cannot move

Write down the date the packed product must be available. Then mark the steps that come before it, in reverse order:

1. Product on sale or delivered to the customer
2. Packing your product into the boxes
3. Packaging delivered to your warehouse or co-packer
4. Shipping and customs clearance
5. Mass production and quality check
6. Sample approval
7. Proofing and dieline sign-off
8. Design and brief

## Phase 1: Brief

A clear brief saves the most time later. Before contacting a manufacturer, collect:

- **What goes inside.** Product size and weight, how many items per box, and whether the set changes between variants.
- **Quantity.** Estimated quantity per design and whether there will be a re-order. The minimum order quantity for each structure is {{待Ryan确认}}.
- **Structure.** A rigid gift box, a folding carton, a sleeve over an existing box or an advent calendar with compartments.
- **Look and feel.** Paper, colours, foil or embossing, and any reference packaging you like.
- **Delivery.** Where the boxes need to arrive and in what form: assembled or flat.

## Phase 2: Design and structure

Seasonal designs often change late, because marketing wants the final campaign artwork. Agree on the structure first, so the dieline is ready while the artwork is still in progress.

- Choose the box style and insert before the artwork is final. Changing the structure after proofing usually restarts the schedule.
- Keep the number of design variants realistic. Each extra variant adds proofs and checks.
- For advent calendars, decide early how the compartments are filled and closed. The insert design drives both the cost and the packing time.

### Advent calendar packaging ideas

- A book-style rigid box with numbered doors and a fitted insert
- A drawer or tower structure with individual small boxes
- A flat folding carton with perforated windows for lighter items

## Phase 3: Artwork and proofing

Once the dieline is approved, the artwork is placed on it and checked as a digital proof. Colour-critical designs may need a physical colour proof as well. The proofing steps we offer: {{待Ryan确认}}.

Common causes of delay at this stage:

- Text or logos too close to folds and cut lines
- Brand colours that need a spot colour instead of CMYK
- Last-minute changes to legal text or barcodes

## Phase 4: Sampling

A physical sample shows how the box opens, how the product fits and how the finishes look together. Plan time to review it with everyone who has to approve the packaging, not just the designer. The sample lead time is {{待Ryan确认}}, and the sample cost policy is {{待Ryan确认}}.

If the sample needs changes, a second sample may be required. Build that possibility into the plan instead of hoping the first sample is perfect.

## Phase 6: Shipping and delivery

Shipping is the step that most often eats the buffer. Agree on the shipping method and terms in the quote: {{待Ryan确认}}. For deliveries to Russia, customs clearance and the documents required are {{待Ryan确认}}. Flat-packed boxes take less space; non-collapsible rigid boxes usually ship assembled, while collapsible rigid structures may ship flat. Confirm the shipping form for your chosen structure. Allow time for the boxes to be received and packed at your warehouse before the launch date.

## Build in buffers

Each phase can slip: artwork arrives late, a sample needs a second round, a shipment waits at a port. Add a buffer to each phase rather than one at the end, and decide in advance what you can simplify if time runs short, for example fewer finishes or fewer design variants.

## Summary

Start from the launch date, list every step backwards, lock the structure before the artwork, and add a buffer to each phase. A schedule built this way survives the late changes that every holiday season brings.
